In one line: We teach AI to spot and follow small objects in orbit — satellites and debris — from a camera in space.

This project is part of the Korea Space Situational Awareness (K-SSA) Program, funded by the Korea Astronomy and Space Science Institute (KASI). KASI will launch two small satellites, SpaceOWL-1 and SpaceOWL-2, that watch the sky for objects in orbit. York University builds the AI that finds those objects in the SpaceOWL images and follows them from frame to frame. The project runs for 48 months, from November 2026 to October 2030.

Why it matters

Orbits are getting crowded. A single collision can create thousands of new pieces of debris. Seeing objects early gives satellite operators time to move out of the way.

Space situational awareness works like a chain:

  1. Detect an object in an image.
  2. Track it across frames.
  3. Determine its orbit.
  4. Assess the collision risk.
  5. Avoid the collision.

York is responsible for the first two links: detection and tracking. Every later step depends on them. A missed detection is a missed warning. A false detection is a false alarm.

Why this needs AI

Finding objects in space images is hard for four reasons. Each one shapes part of our work.

ChallengeWhat it meansOur approach
Tiny targetsA satellite is often only 1–3 pixels wide, among thousands of stars.Networks that look at several frames together. Stars move together; objects in orbit move differently.
No real images before launchSpaceOWL has not flown yet, so there are no real images to train on.A physics-based simulator, plus real images from earlier missions.
Little data after launchA satellite can send only a small part of its images to the ground.Methods that learn from a few real examples without forgetting what they already know.
SpeedImages must be processed in near real time.One shared network for detection and tracking, inside an automated pipeline.

The plan: Build, Fly, Adapt

The project runs in three stages:

  • Build (2026–2028): develop the data and AI models, up to a flight-ready version 1.0.
  • Fly (2029): freeze the models, test them with the ground system, and support the SpaceOWL-1 launch.
  • Adapt (2030): improve the models with real flight images. SpaceOWL-2 follows in early 2030.

The core idea is a closed sim-to-real loop. Real images make the simulator more realistic. A better simulator trains better models. The models are then checked on real images again. Before launch the loop runs on real images from earlier missions. After launch it runs on SpaceOWL’s own images.

Build (2026–2028)

Work-A: AI-ready data

Led by Prof. Regina Lee. The goal is enough good data for the AI to learn before launch.

  • A-1 Physics-based simulator. We render synthetic star-tracker images step by step: real star catalogues (Gaia) and satellite orbits, viewing geometry, sunlight on each object, camera optics, and detector noise. The simulator knows where every object is, so every image comes with exact labels for free. The target is at least 100,000 synthetic frames, in both survey and tracking modes.
  • A-2 Image enhancement. Cleaning images without erasing the faint 1–3 pixel targets.
  • A-3 Sim-to-real adaptation. Making what the AI learns on synthetic images work on real ones.
  • A-4 Benchmark and labels. A real-image test set. An AI agent proposes labels and flags objects that may have been missed; a person confirms them. The target is at least 10,000 machine-labelled and 2,000 human-checked frames.

We already have three kinds of images to start with:

  • From orbit. The DUST benchmark: 1,378 star-tracker frames with 4,237 labelled objects from 22 observation sessions, taken by the Fast Auroral Imager on the CASSIOPE satellite. We also use wide-field image sequences from the same camera.
  • From the stratosphere. Star-tracker cameras flown on high-altitude balloons (RSONAR 1 and 2) through the Canadian Space Agency’s Strato-Science campaigns. In August 2023, four cameras flew at about 37 km and recorded objects in orbit.
  • Synthetic. Our DUST-like generator, which copies the look of real DUST images, and a star-tracker simulator from Universidad Politécnica de Madrid (IDR/UPM).

Work-B: Detection and tracking

Led by Prof. Gunho Sohn.

  • B-1 Detection. Find each object and measure its position to a fraction of a pixel, then convert it to sky coordinates.
  • B-2 Tracking. Link detections across frames into one track per object during a single pass. The tracker predicts where the object should be next, keeps a track alive when the object briefly disappears (for example, behind a bright star), and checks that each track follows a physically sensible path.
  • B-3 SpaceOWL vision network. One shared network for detection and tracking on both satellites. New tasks can be added later with small add-on modules.

Each finished track is handed to the Korean orbit-determination team in an agreed format. It includes time, position, uncertainty, which satellite took the image, and quality flags. We are designing this format together with that team.

Early results

Our methods already meet the program’s minimum targets on real space images. Precision is the share of detections that are real objects. Recall is the share of real objects that we find.

MeasureProgram minimumOur result
Detection precision85%97.2% (wide-field CASSIOPE images)
Detection recall70%74.2% (wide-field CASSIOPE images)
Tracking accuracy (MOTA)70%71.4% (wide-field CASSIOPE images)

Training first on synthetic video helps. On the real DUST images, a network pre-trained on our synthetic sequences raised detection F1 from 80.1% to 84.4% and found 7–9% more faint objects. These are early results on a validation set; an independent test is still to come.

The hardest cases remain objects of 4 pixels or less, slow movers, and objects near bright stars. These are our focus for Years 2 and 3.

Fly (2029)

In 2029 we tune the models on the latest camera data, test them inside the ground system, and release a flight-frozen version. We then rehearse full passes end to end. SpaceOWL-1 is planned to launch in late 2029. When the first real images come down, we compare them with our synthetic images to measure the gap.

York hands over the flight-frozen models, documented software interfaces, ready-to-run software containers, test suites, and model documentation.

Adapt (2030)

Led by Prof. Shweta Mahajan. After launch, only a small set of real images reaches the ground. We use it to:

  • C-1 compare real and synthetic images, and recalibrate the simulator;
  • C-2 fine-tune the models on a few real examples, while keeping what they learned before launch;
  • C-3 validate the adapted models on orbit, and transfer them to SpaceOWL-2.

The loop repeats each time new flight data arrives.
